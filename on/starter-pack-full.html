Part 1

The Compliance Calendar

The recurring obligations a board has to hit, in one place. Each one cites the section it rests on and the free tool that does the math.

Annual general meeting

6 months after year end

The board must hold an AGM within six months of the end of each fiscal year, and a new corporation holds its first one not more than three months after the declaration and description are registered. Six months is counted under the Legislation Act, 2006: the count leaves out the month the year ends in and lands on the same day number, or the last day of a shorter month. A December 31 year end gives June 30; a June 30 year end gives December 30. A deadline that lands on a holiday moves to the next day that is not one, and Saturday is not a holiday. Work the notices back from the meeting date (next entry), and attach the financial statements and the auditor’s report to the notice of the AGM.

Condominium Act, 1998, ss. 45(2), 69(1)–(2) · Legislation Act, 2006, ss. 88(2), 89(1), 89(6)

Source: knowyourstrata.com/on/agm-deadline — verified 16 October 2026

Preliminary Notice & Notice of Meeting

35 days, combined

The Notice of Meeting must be given at least 15 days before the day of the meeting. Before that notice can go out, a Preliminary Notice must be given at least 20 days before it. For any board-called meeting — an AGM, a turn-over meeting, or a meeting called for other business — that means the Preliminary Notice needs to go out at least 35 days ahead of the meeting date. Neither figure changes by meeting type.

Condominium Act, 1998, ss. 47(1)(b)–(c)

Source: knowyourstrata.com/on/notice-of-meeting-deadline — verified 19 August 2026

Owner-requisitioned meeting

35 days, or 20 + 40 from Dec 31

Owners holding at least 15% of the units may requisition a meeting. Which clock runs depends on the day the board received the requisition (O. Reg. 48/01 s. 80).

Received before December 31, 2026: the board must call and hold the meeting within 35 days (or add the business to the next AGM, if the requisitionists agree). If the board doesn't act, a requisitionist may call the meeting themselves, to be held within 45 days of doing so.

Received on or after December 31, 2026: the board answers in writing within 20 days that it will, or will not, call the meeting, with reasons if not. Silence counts as agreeing. Once it agrees, the meeting must be held within 40 days after the 20 day response period ends, or the business added to the next AGM scheduled at least 40 days after that period ends, if the requisitionists ask or consent. There is no longer a self-help meeting: after a refusal the requisitionists revise within 10 days, or apply to the Condominium Authority Tribunal or the Superior Court within 20 days.

Under either version the 15-day/20-day notice minimums above still apply to the meeting itself.

Condominium Act, 1998, ss. 46(1), 46(4)(b), 46(5) (before December 31, 2026); s. 46(7), (10), (11), (13), (15) as replaced, O. Reg. 48/01 ss. 12.2.3, 80 (from that day)

Source: knowyourstrata.com/on/requisition-threshold and /on/notice-of-meeting-deadline — verified 20 & 19 August 2026; the December 31, 2026 clock read 27 October 2026, see /on/december-31-2026-changes

Reserved board seat request (from December 31, 2026)

15 days after the preliminary notice

For a meeting whose preliminary notice goes out on or after December 31, 2026, the reserved seat follows non-leased voting units (residential, owner entitled to vote, not leased in the 60 days before the notice) instead of owner-occupied ones. When the reserved position is vacant or about to be, the preliminary notice must carry the definition, the text of s. 51(5) and (6), and the last day of a 15 day window. Within those 15 days an owner of a non-leased voting unit may deliver a signed written statement and a request that someone be elected to the seat. The seat is reserved only if a request arrives in time, at least one unit is a non-leased voting unit, and a minority of the units are. A reserved position that exists on December 30 carries on.

Condominium Act, 1998, ss. 1(1), 45.1(1)(a.1), 51(5), (6) as amended December 31, 2026; O. Reg. 48/01 ss. 12.2(7), (8), 79, 81

Source: knowyourstrata.com/on/december-31-2026-changes — read 27 October 2026

Records request — board's response

30 days

The board must determine whether to allow access and respond to the requester in writing within 30 days of receiving a request for records. That response is the board's answer, not the record itself — delivery of the record runs on a separate clock, depending on whether it's a core record and how it's being delivered: the same 30 days if it's a core record delivered electronically, 7 days after payment if a core record on paper, or 30 days after payment for anything else.

O. Reg. 48/01, s. 13.3(6); ss. 13.4–13.7

Source: knowyourstrata.com/on/records-request-deadline — verified 19 August 2026

Reserve fund study

1 year, then every 3

The first reserve fund study is due within a year of registering the declaration and description, and must be a comprehensive study. Every study after that is due within 3 years of the previous study's completion — a rolling clock measured from the last study, not a fixed calendar date. The class required alternates between an updated study with and without a site inspection; it does not loop back to a full comprehensive study on any set cycle.

Condominium Act, 1998, s. 94(4) · O. Reg. 48/01, ss. 31(2)–(4)

Source: knowyourstrata.com/on/reserve-fund-study-deadline — verified 20 August 2026

After the reserve fund study lands

120 → 15 → 30 days

Once the board receives the study, it has 120 days to review it and propose a funding plan. From there, 15 more days to notify owners (a summary) and the auditor (the full study and plan). Then 30 more days after that notice before the board must implement the plan.

Condominium Act, 1998, s. 94(8)–(10)

Source: knowyourstrata.com/on/reserve-fund-study-deadline — verified 20 August 2026

Changing the common elements

30 days, or a 66 2/3% vote

A change to the common elements is not substantial unless its estimated cost exceeds 10 per cent of the annual budgeted common expenses for the current fiscal year. Below that line the board gives notice under s. 97(3), with the estimated cost, the funding, a statement of the s. 46 requisition right and copies of ss. 46 and 97, and may proceed if no meeting is requisitioned within 30 days. Above it, or where the board elects to treat the change as substantial, owners of at least 66 2/3 per cent of the units must vote in favour at a meeting called for the purpose. There is no prescribed amount under s. 97(6)(a)(ii), so the 10 per cent figure is the whole test.

Condominium Act, 1998, s. 97(2)–(6) · O. Reg. 48/01, s. 24.3

Source: knowyourstrata.com/on/voting-threshold — verified 12 September 2026

Common expense arrears: the lien clock

3 months, minus 10 days

A lien arises the moment an owner defaults on the common expenses, and it expires three months after that default unless a certificate of lien is registered first. The board does not get the full three months: at least 10 days of written notice must reach the owner before the certificate is registered. Written notice must also reach every encumbrancer registered against the title on or before the registration day, or the lien loses its priority over that encumbrance. A notice given late, after registration, restores that priority only for the arrears from the three months before the notice onward (s. 86(6)).

Condominium Act, 1998, ss. 85(1)–(2), 85(4), 86(3)–(5) · Legislation Act, 2006, ss. 88(2), 89(1)–(3), 89(6)

Source: knowyourstrata.com/on/lien-deadline — verified 22 September 2026

Information certificates

60 days after Q1 and Q3

The periodic information certificate goes to owners twice a year, within 60 days after the last day of the first quarter and of the third quarter of the fiscal year, plus any extra dates a by-law sets. Each one is current as of its quarter end. Between them, an information certificate update is due within 30 days of a listed change (the address for service, directors or officers, the manager, the delivery or email method, or the insurance deductible), or within 5 days when vacancies leave too few directors for quorum. A new owner who has given notice of their name and unit gets a new owner information certificate within 30 days.

Condominium Act, 1998, s. 26.3 · O. Reg. 48/01, ss. 11.1(4)–(6), 11.2(3), 11.3(4)

From December 31, 2026: the periodic certificate also has to identify any director holding the reserved seat under s. 51(6) (O. Reg. 48/01 s. 11.1(1)(d.1), read 27 October 2026). The due dates do not change.

Source: knowyourstrata.com/on/information-certificate-deadline — verified 6 October 2026

Part 4

New Board Member Orientation

A plain-English walk-through of the job's recurring obligations. Every specific figure here is the same one cited in Part 1 — this section doesn't introduce anything new, it just explains it.

Serving on an Ontario condo board mostly means running the same few obligations on repeat, on time, in the right order. The Compliance Calendar in Part 1 is the actual reference; this section is the plain-English version of why each entry matters.

Notice comes in two parts, not one

Before the board can send the Notice of Meeting itself, it has to send a Preliminary Notice first — and that Preliminary Notice needs its own 20-day runway before the Notice of Meeting goes out, which then needs another 15 days before the meeting. Miss the sequencing and the whole 35-day chain slips.

Do the math: knowyourstrata.com/on/notice-of-meeting-deadline

Quorum is a share of units, and the reduced number is narrower than it sounds

Quorum is 25% of the units by default. Only an AGM or a turn-over meeting gets the reduced 15% figure, and only on the third attempt — a requisitioned meeting or anything else stays at a flat 25% no matter how many times it's tried. It's easy to assume the 15% number is more general than it actually is. And the units are the ones that vote: separate parking, storage and service units are left out of the count (O. Reg. 48/01 s. 1.1, read with s. 49(3) of the Act).

Do the math: knowyourstrata.com/on/quorum

An owner requisition has its own threshold and its own clock, and the clock changes on December 31, 2026

Owners holding 15% of the units can force a meeting. For a requisition the board received before December 31, 2026, the board has 35 days to hold it, or a requisitionist can step in and call it themselves within 45 more days. For one received on or after that day the replacement section applies: a prescribed form, a 20 day written answer (silence counts as yes), then 40 days to hold the meeting, and no self-help route. The received date decides, not the meeting date (O. Reg. 48/01 s. 80). The 15% figure is the same under both, and as with quorum, separate parking, storage and service units are left out of it (O. Reg. 48/01 s. 1.1).

Do the math: knowyourstrata.com/on/requisition-threshold

Records requests run on two clocks, not one

The board's 30-day response is just the first step — it says yes or no and states the fee. Actually delivering the record runs on a second clock that depends on whether it's a "core record" (a short, fixed list) and how it's delivered. Treating the 30 days as the whole obligation is the most common way this one gets missed.

Do the math: knowyourstrata.com/on/records-request-deadline

The reserve fund study cycle rolls forward from the last study, and alternates in class

After the first (always comprehensive) study, each later one is due 3 years after the previous study's completion — not a fixed date on the calendar. The class alternates between two types of "updated" study and never cycles back to a full comprehensive study on its own. A "comprehensive study every six years" rule doesn't actually exist in the text.

Do the math: knowyourstrata.com/on/reserve-fund-study-deadline

A by-law needs a majority of all units, except the ones that do not

Section 56(10) says a by-law is not effective until the owners of a majority of the units vote to confirm it — and then adds “or such other number of owners that is prescribed”. That prescription is real. O. Reg. 48/01 s. 14(2) sets a majority of those present or by proxy for a short, specific list of by-laws. Applying the wrong one either fails a by-law you actually passed or registers one you did not.

Do the math: knowyourstrata.com/on/voting-threshold

The deductible chargeback reaches the owner’s own unit, and stops there

Everyone remembers that an owner who causes damage pays the deductible. Section 105(2) actually says that about damage to the owner’s unit. A burst hose that soaks the corridor or the unit below is not caught by it at all — that stays a common expense unless a by-law under s. 105(3) extends the chargeback. And the amount is the lesser of the repair cost and the deductible, not the deductible every time.

Do the math: knowyourstrata.com/on/insurance-deductible

Common expenses come from the declaration, and there is no fallback formula

British Columbia has a statutory default: unit entitlement under s. 99 of the Strata Property Act. Ontario has nothing of the kind. Section 84(1) says owners contribute in the proportions specified in the declaration, and stops there. If the declaration is not in front of you, the Act cannot tell you the answer. An equal split is common, but it is a fact about a particular declaration rather than a rule you may assume.

Two things follow. An owner cannot withhold contributions because they have stopped using the common elements, are in dispute with the corporation, or are restricted from using something — s. 84(3) closes all three arguments. And a surplus is not refundable: it goes to future common expenses or into the reserve fund, never back to owners except on termination.

Do the math: knowyourstrata.com/on/common-expense-calculator

The reserve floor is ten per cent of the budget with the reserve taken out

Until a corporation has conducted a first reserve fund study and implemented a funding plan, s. 93(5) fixes the reserve contribution at the greater of the s. 93(6) amount and ten per cent of the budgeted common expenses exclusive of the reserve fund. Those last four words are the whole rule. On a $500,000 budget carrying a $50,000 reserve line the floor is ten per cent of $450,000, which is $45,000, not $50,000. Both readings look reasonable on the page and only one of them is the statute.

Check yours: knowyourstrata.com/on/common-expense-calculator

The lien clock is three months, and the owner notice eats ten days of it

A lien on unpaid common expenses expires three months after the default unless a certificate of lien is registered. Boards read that as three months of runway. It is not: s. 85(4) requires at least ten days of written notice to the owner before registering, so the date to act on is three months minus ten days. Discover the arrears on day eighty-five and it is already too late to register in time.

The step most often missed is the one that costs the most. Section 86(3) requires written notice to every encumbrancer registered against the title, on or before the day of registration, and s. 86(5) says the lien loses its priority over an encumbrance without it. Register perfectly on time, tell nobody but the owner, and the mortgage still gets paid first. If the notice was missed, send it anyway: under s. 86(6) a late notice restores priority for the arrears from the three months before it onward, plus interest and reasonable collection costs, though not for anything older.

Work out the dates: knowyourstrata.com/on/lien-deadline

The AGM deadline is six months, not 180 days

Section 45(2) gives the board six months from the fiscal year end to hold the AGM, and the Legislation Act counts months by calendar date, not by days. That puts a December 31 year end on June 30 and a June 30 year end on December 30. Work back from the meeting date: the notice of meeting 15 days before, the preliminary notice 20 days before that, and the financial statements and auditor’s report attached to the notice.

Do the math: knowyourstrata.com/on/agm-deadline

Information certificates are twice a year, not every quarter

The Act says every three months unless the regulation prescribes otherwise, and it does: within 60 days after the first and third quarters. Updates run on their own 30 and 5 day clocks. Missing the update after a director change is the usual slip.

Do the math: knowyourstrata.com/on/information-certificate-deadline

The reserved board seat follows non-leased units from December 31, 2026

Today one board seat is reserved for owner-occupied units when at least 15% of the units are owner-occupied. For a meeting whose preliminary notice goes out on or after December 31, 2026, the test becomes non-leased voting units, the seat has to be asked for within 15 days of the preliminary notice, and it is reserved only while those units are a minority. A seat that already exists on December 30 continues (O. Reg. 48/01 ss. 79, 81). If the seat is vacant or about to be, the preliminary notice has more to say than it used to.

Read the detail: knowyourstrata.com/on/december-31-2026-changes

Ontario condominium law changes in stages — always check the date

This pack gives you the law as verified on the dates shown, and since 27 October 2026 it carries the December 31, 2026 requisition and reserved seat rules beside the current ones. Check which side of that date your requisition or preliminary notice falls on, and confirm anything time-sensitive against the current text before relying on it for something that matters.
